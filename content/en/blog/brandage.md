Paul Graham is the co-founder of Y Combinator and one of the great philosophers of the computer science world. For 25 years he has been publishing thoughtful essays on his [blog](https://www.paulgraham.com/articles.html) — though infrequently: in 2025, for instance, he published just four pieces. In March 2026 a new one appeared: [«The Brand Age»](https://www.paulgraham.com/brandage.html). I'd like to discuss it.

Why does this matter to me? Among the writers [I follow](/people/), Paul Graham comes first. I've referenced his essays several times before — in my roundup of [resources for product managers](/pm-materials/), in [«The 10 Deadly Sins of a Product Manager»](/10-evils/), and in [«Thinking Through Writing»](/text/). I'm also drawn to his style: clarifying footnotes and acknowledgments to well-known practitioners who reviewed his drafts. In this latest essay, the reviewers include Sam Altman, among others. And equally important — there's no advertising, no promotion of any services.

Here are the key ideas from "The Brand Age" as I see them.

1. Every technology company has a golden age — a period when technological advantage is the core value driver for the business. But that time always ends.

2. In the technology race, all solutions converge: products become essentially identical. The essay's example is Swiss wristwatches, which by the late 1960s all looked the same because every manufacturer was focused on improving just two metrics: accuracy and thinness. Graham describes the watches of that era as "quiet perfection."

3. This is the essence of minimalism — all solutions to a given problem converge toward a single one. If I find a great solution, it will be copied. That's why the design and logic of many services look so similar.

4. You see this principle everywhere. Take the current LLM race, where models fight for every fraction of a percent on benchmarks. By and large, all major LLMs perform at similar quality and are in constant competition with one another.

5. When all solutions become alike and quality converges, the "nerd" companies that keep treating quality as the ultimate differentiator — die.

6. Brand is what remains when the substantive differences between products disappear. The survivors are those who make the bet on branding at the right time.

7. Good design, like mathematics, seeks the correct answer — and correct answers tend to converge. Branding, however, is orthogonal to good design. Graham says it plainly: there is a fundamental, unavoidable conflict between branding and design.

8. To me, this means that at some point a company must stop focusing on product quality and start focusing on recognition. To survive. For anyone close to technology, that's not a comfortable conclusion. But that's what the brand age is.

9. Once building a brand and driving recognition becomes the primary business objective, design loses its logic and turns strange.

10. To avoid ending up in a world of dead design and dead technology — the brand age — Graham suggests following interesting problems ("follow the problems"), so you stay in the golden age.

So, fellow product managers — let's stop wasting time on nonsense and start looking for real, interesting problems.
