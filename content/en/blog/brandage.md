Paul Graham is the co-founder of Y Combinator and one of the foremost philosophers of the Computer Science world. For 25 years he has been publishing thoughtful essays on his [blog](https://www.paulgraham.com/articles.html) — though rarely: in 2025, for instance, he published just four pieces. In March 2026 a new one appeared — [«The Brand Age»](https://www.paulgraham.com/brandage.html). I'd like to discuss it.

Why does this matter to me? Among the authors [I follow](/people/), Paul Graham sits at the top. I've referenced his essays several times before — in my roundup of [resources for product managers](/pm-materials/), in [«The 10 Deadly Sins of a Product Manager»](/10-evils/), and in [«Thinking Through Writing»](/text/). I'm also drawn to his style: clarifying footnotes and acknowledgments to well-known specialists who review his drafts. In this latest essay, for example, Sam Altman is among the reviewers. And an equally important factor — no ads, no promotion of any services.

Here are the key ideas from "The Brand Age" as I see them.

1. Every technology company has its golden age — a window of time when technological advantage is the core value driving the business. But that window always closes.

2. In the technology race, all solutions converge — products become virtually identical. Graham's example: Swiss wristwatches by the late 1960s all looked the same, because every manufacturer was focused on improving just two metrics: accuracy and thinness. Graham describes the watches of that era as "quiet perfection."

3. This is the essence of minimalism — all solutions to a given problem converge on a single one. If I find a great solution, it will be copied. That's why the design and logic of so many services look remarkably alike.

4. This principle shows up everywhere. Take the current LLM race, where models fight over every percentage point on benchmarks. Broadly speaking, all the well-known LLMs perform at similar quality levels and are in constant competition with one another.

5. When all solutions become equivalent in quality, the nerdy companies that keep treating quality as the ultimate differentiator — die.

6. Brand is what remains when meaningful differences between products disappear. The survivors are those who place their bet on branding at the right moment.

7. Good design, like mathematics, searches for the correct answer — and correct answers tend to converge. Branding, however, is orthogonal to good design. Graham says it plainly: there is a fundamental conflict between branding and design that cannot be avoided.

8. To me, this means that at some point a company will have to stop obsessing over product quality and start focusing on recognition. To survive. For someone who lives close to technology, that's not a comfortable idea. But that's the essence of the brand age.

9. When building a brand and achieving recognition become the primary business goal, design loses its logic and starts to look strange.

10. To avoid ending up in a world of dead design and stale technology — the brand age — Graham proposes following interesting problems: "follow the problems," so that you stay in the golden age.

So, fellow product managers — let's stop wasting time on nonsense and start looking for genuinely interesting problems.
