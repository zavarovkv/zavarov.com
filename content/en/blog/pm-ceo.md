Technology today is advancing at an accelerating pace — there's even a concept called "technological singularity," suggesting that in some hypothetical future, explosive technological growth will fundamentally transform our civilization.

But it's worth remembering that technology doesn't enter our lives on its own. It arrives through IT products, built to solve specific problems or address the needs of a particular audience.

Product managers play one of the central roles in this process. They identify real audience problems, devise the best solutions, create new user habits, shape new markets, and articulate new ideas that will remain relevant in the future.

<h2>The Product Manager's Role in a Company — and Their Toolkit</h2>

Product management is a multifaceted profession sitting at the intersection of business, technology, and research. A PM works on the product from every angle: analyzing the market and competitors, researching the target audience and their needs, testing hypotheses, shaping the vision, and managing technical and creative teams to execute the strategy. What's more, a product manager is the CEO of their product — their decisions determine its financial success.

The process of building a new product can be loosely divided into two stages: "problem discovery and solution finding," followed by "development and launch." In modern terminology, these are called Discovery and Delivery — a framework borrowed from design thinking and the Double Diamond model. Unlike most other members of a product team, the PM is actively involved in both stages.

A strong PM has an extensive toolkit of techniques and methods they apply every day. When launching a new product or a major feature during the Discovery phase, for instance, they'll assess potential market size using the TAM/SAM/SOM model (TAM — total addressable market; SAM — serviceable addressable market; SOM — serviceable obtainable market), and analyze competitors' strengths and weaknesses through SWOT analysis or a competition map.

At every stage of the product lifecycle, the PM researches current and potential audiences — their characteristics, goals, and needs. The most common qualitative research tools include in-depth interviews, surveys, feedback analysis, and UX research. The Jobs To Be Done (JTBD) framework has also gained significant traction for uncovering people's deeper motivations and needs.

A PM also needs to be fluent in quantitative research: web analytics and A/B testing. The importance of these skills grows with the size of the active user base, since it becomes possible to mathematically measure the impact of changes on product and business metrics.

More often than not, PMs tackle research and analytical tasks not alone but alongside a team of specialists — designers, UX researchers, and product analysts. Though this isn't always the case; the process depends on company size and organizational structure.

Beyond research, the PM plays an active role in product development: managing the backlog and prioritization (using frameworks like RICE or ICE), writing requirements (User Stories, Definition of Done), and helping organize processes, motivate teams, and support hiring.

That's why soft skills are just as important. The PM is the critical link in communication between research teams, technical teams, and the business.

<h2>Product Value — What It Is and How to Define It</h2>

A product's value is determined, first and foremost, by whether it solves a user problem better than the alternatives. If it does, the majority of new users will stick around and become a regular active audience, and the product will grow. This state of having found genuine value for users is known in product management as achieving Product-Market Fit.

This gives the PM their foundational objective: finding and achieving Product-Market Fit for a specific user segment. To get there, the manager and team must navigate a challenging path — studying the market, the users, and their problems; testing multiple value proposition hypotheses; building an MVP (or MLP — Minimum Lovable Product); observing user reactions; and repeating this cycle multiple times.

Second, a product must also deliver value to the business. But true value is only achieved when there's both Product-Market Fit and a favorable customer acquisition cost (CAC) — ideally one that's several times lower than the revenue a user generates over their lifetime.

<h2>Balancing Creativity with Objectivity</h2>

The word "creativity" might conjure images of eccentric artists. But let's think more broadly. Creativity is the ability to break from convention and find new ways to solve pressing problems. In that sense, it's one of the most important skills a PM can have.

A hallmark of a great product manager is the ability to identify the root cause of a user problem, find the most effective solution, and execute it. Experience, broad exposure to products and ideas, and creative thinking all contribute to this.

Approaching problems creatively helps at every stage — whether you're generating a hypothesis, testing it quickly, building an MVP, optimizing a business process, or discovering a new user acquisition channel. Innovation and inventiveness are welcome at every step of a product's evolution. So there's no need to worry that AI will replace this profession anytime soon — it won't.

Equally important is the ability to measure the results of product decisions in concrete numbers. That's what keeps the work grounded in objectivity: if business and product metrics are growing, the manager is making the right calls.

<h2>How a Product Manager Creates New Meaning</h2>

The 21st century is the age of information technology — a kind of universal clay from which new technological products are constantly being shaped. We've grown accustomed to an internet-connected life, to distributed work, and to remote collaboration. We've learned to experience the world through screens. The digitized way of life we inhabit today is largely the result of technology permeating our daily routines.

In this way, technology — or more precisely, technological products — gives rise to new habits, new social behaviors, new values, and new meaning. Product managers are directly involved in this process, as the authors and creators of those products.

Information technology is far from the only driver of new values and meaning. Another important factor is the team building the product. Most of the digital services we use every day are created by cross-cultural groups — which means they inevitably carry multicultural values. And product managers are often involved in hiring and shaping those teams.

In closing, I want to emphasize once more that a product manager is a multifaceted professional who manages both technical and creative teams inside the company while actively engaging with an external audience. Drawing on broad exposure, accumulated experience, and the value generated by their teams, a PM formulates the product vision and charts its development strategy. That's why it matters to continuously develop both hard and soft skills, cultivate broad awareness, sharpen creative problem-solving, listen carefully to your users, and always advocate for them.
