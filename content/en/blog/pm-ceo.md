Technology today is advancing at a breakneck pace. There's even a concept called "technological singularity" — the idea that in some hypothetical future, explosive technological growth will fundamentally transform our civilization.

But it's worth remembering that technology doesn't enter our lives on its own. It arrives through IT products — products built to solve specific problems or meet the particular needs of their audiences.

Product managers play one of the most pivotal roles in this process. They identify the real problems people face, devise the best possible solutions, shape new user habits, build new markets, and articulate new ideas that will resonate well into the future.

<h2>The Product Manager's Role in a Company — and the Tools They Use</h2>

Product management is a multifaceted profession that sits at the intersection of business, technology, and research. A product manager takes a holistic view of the product: analyzing the market and competitors, studying the target audience and their needs, validating hypotheses, shaping the vision, and managing technical and creative teams to execute the strategy. In this sense, a product manager is the CEO of their product — their decisions directly determine its financial success.

The process of creating a new product can be roughly divided into two stages: "problem exploration and solution discovery," followed by "development and delivery." In modern terminology, these are called "Discovery" and "Delivery." This approach has its roots in design thinking and the Double Diamond model. Unlike other members of the product team, the product manager is actively involved in both stages.

A strong product manager has an extensive toolkit of techniques they draw on every day. When launching a new product or a major feature, for example, they'll use the TAM/SAM/SOM framework during Discovery to estimate potential market size (TAM — Total Addressable Market; SAM — Serviceable Addressable Market; SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market). They'll analyze competitors' strengths and weaknesses through SWOT analysis or by building a Competition Map.

At every stage of the product lifecycle, the manager researches current and potential audiences — their characteristics, goals, and needs. The most common qualitative research tools include in-depth interviews, surveys, feedback analysis, and UX research. The Jobs To Be Done (JTBD) framework has also become widely popular for uncovering the deeper motivations and needs that drive user behavior.

A product manager also needs to be fluent in quantitative research: web analytics and A/B testing are essential. The importance of these skills grows with the size of the active user base, since a larger audience makes it possible to statistically measure the impact of changes on both product and business metrics.

In practice, product managers rarely tackle research and analytics tasks alone — they work alongside designers, UX researchers, and product analysts. That said, the specific setup depends on the size of the company and its organizational structure.

Beyond research, the product manager is deeply involved in product development: managing the backlog and priorities (using frameworks like RICE or ICE), writing requirements (User Stories, Definition of Done), and helping with process organization, team motivation, and hiring.

That's why soft skills matter just as much. The product manager is the central link in communication between research teams, engineering teams, and the business.

<h2>Product Value — What It Is and How to Define It</h2>

A product's value is determined, first and foremost, by whether it solves a user problem better than the alternatives. When it does, the majority of new users stick around and become a loyal, active audience — and the product grows. In product management terms, this state of having found genuine value for users is called achieving Product Market Fit.

This is the fundamental challenge for any product manager: finding and achieving Product Market Fit for a specific user segment. To get there, the manager and team have to travel a demanding road — studying the market, the users, and their problems; testing multiple value proposition hypotheses; building an MVP (or MLP — Minimum Lovable Product); analyzing user reactions; and repeating that cycle more than once.

The product also needs to deliver business value. But true value is only achieved when Product Market Fit is in place and the cost of acquiring a new user (Customer Acquisition Cost) is a fraction of the revenue that user generates.

<h2>Balancing Creativity with Objectivity</h2>

The word "creativity" might conjure images of eccentric artists. But let's think about it more broadly. Creativity is the ability to step outside conventional rules and find new ways to tackle real problems. In that sense, it's one of the most essential skills a product manager can have.

A great product manager knows how to identify the root cause of a user problem, find an optimal and effective solution, and bring it to life. Experience, broad awareness of the product landscape, and creative thinking all contribute to that ability.

Thinking creatively about problems helps at every turn — whether you're generating a hypothesis and testing it quickly, building an MVP, streamlining a business process, or finding a new user acquisition channel. Innovation and creativity are welcome at every stage of a product's development. So there's little reason to worry that AI will replace the role anytime soon — it won't.

Equally important is the ability to measure the results of product decisions in concrete numbers. That's what keeps the work grounded in objectivity: when business and product metrics are moving in the right direction, it's a signal that the manager is making the right calls.

<h2>How Product Managers Create New Meaning</h2>

The 21st century is the age of information technology — a universal raw material for building new technological products. We've grown accustomed to an internet-connected life, to distributed work, and to interacting with each other remotely. We've learned to experience the world through screens. The digitized way we live today is, in large part, a direct result of technology advancing and embedding itself into our everyday routines.

In this way, technology — or more precisely, technological products — generates new habits, new social behaviors, new values, and new meaning. And product managers are directly involved in that process, as the authors and creators of those products.

Information technology isn't the only vehicle for creating new values and meaning through products. Equally important is the team behind a product's development. Most of the digital services we use every day are built by cross-cultural groups — which means they inevitably carry multicultural values. And product managers often play a direct role in hiring and shaping those teams.

To sum up: a product manager is a multifunctional professional who leads technical and creative teams within a company while actively engaging with the external audience. Drawing on their own breadth of experience and the accumulated value created by their teams, they shape the product vision and define its strategic direction. That's why it's so important to keep developing both hard and soft skills, to stay curious and widely informed, to hone creative problem-solving — and above all, to listen carefully to users and to be their strongest advocate.
