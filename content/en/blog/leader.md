**— Konstantin, why do you think being a leader is worthwhile or interesting?**

— Let's start with what leadership actually is. There are many different definitions — Peter Drucker, for instance, wrote: "Leadership is the ability to help a person move beyond their own limitations."

Leadership is the skill of positively influencing people in a way that enables a group to achieve ambitious goals through the contribution of each individual member.

The core responsibilities of a leader, accordingly, are: first, to help each person reach beyond their current capabilities, and second, to enable different people to work together as productively as possible.

I'd reframe the question slightly: why is being a leader both interesting *and* difficult? Because the path of leadership is, first and foremost, a path of self-work and self-discovery. Ichak Adizes identifies several qualities of a leader, and all of them are personal traits: the ability to act deliberately and consciously; to understand one's own strengths and weaknesses; to read people well.

This brings me to another definition I use:

<div style="font-weight: 200; font-size:1.25em; line-height:1.5;">Leadership is part of personal growth and self-improvement — an endless process of working on yourself and inspiring the people around you.</div>

<br>

**— Can you be a leader within a team or in a situation where you're not in charge?**

— A leader and a manager in an organization are not the same thing. A leader inspires, creates a vision of the future, initiates change, and makes decisions that move the organization toward its goals. It's also worth emphasizing that a leader is an expert in a specific domain. A manager, on the other hand, follows job descriptions and optimizes existing processes.

So no, you don't have to hold a managerial title in the org chart — though a good leader is also a good manager. Personally, I'm more drawn to the role of an informal leader, since it offers more freedom and independence from organizational structure and corporate politics.

<br>

**— What does it mean to "act like a leader"? How does it show up in practice — in how someone gets results or asks questions?**

— There are many different leadership styles, ranging from authoritarian to laissez-faire (minimal involvement). The communication approach will vary depending on the chosen style. But in general, a leader asks open-ended questions to understand someone's perspective and the reasoning behind it. Steering people toward a predetermined answer using SPIN questions or similar techniques isn't really the done thing in tech. You want to hear the actual answer, not a specific one you've already decided on.

When it comes to results, a leader is someone with a very clear vision of the future. They drive execution by working with individual motivation and by building an effective team united around a shared goal. They also maintain a mature, adult stance — no micromanagement. Especially since nobody appreciates close supervision and unsolicited advice. A leader also doesn't shy away from conflict or try to avoid it — they treat it as a problem to be solved.

<br>

**— How does a leader give feedback?**

— You often see managers or first-time team leads who handle this really poorly: instead of motivating people and giving useful feedback, they end up demotivating their team. A leader's goal is to make the employee *want* to adjust their approach and improve their performance on their own. Say someone is falling behind compared to their peers, and a performance review is coming up — it's already clear they won't get a high rating. If the root cause is gaps in knowledge, I usually share my own tools and techniques, because after years in tech I've tried a lot of things and can point to what actually works. You won't find that in books.

<br>

**— What else is important to understand about leadership?**

— A leader is proactive and engaged — they don't sit around waiting for their manager to tell them what to do. In today's tech industry, you simply can't afford to wait. The pay is good, and in return people expect you to be fully switched on. The more rigid the hierarchy, the less creative energy, initiative, and motivation you'll find.

At Practicum (an online education platform), I work with students transitioning into tech from all kinds of fields, and I see how differently people think about management and leadership. In one workshop, I gave them this scenario: an employee is underperforming, something's clearly going on in their life, but they're not saying what — figure out what to do. Many people coming from more traditional industries treated the employee as a resource, as a task-executor who owes the company their output. They said so directly, cited job responsibilities, behaved in a very formal, directive way. Those who were already closer to the tech world tended to try to start a friendly conversation — to get the employee to open up and share what was going on. They asked questions, showed interest, openness, empathy. I think it's a very telling example.

**— The big question: where do you start on the path to leadership?**

— With working on yourself. Here are the steps I'd recommend.

Develop the habit of self-organization and establish your own time management principles to build a personal productivity system. When I was younger, I studied books by Gleb Arkhangelsky and David Allen on GTD, looking for the tools that would actually work for me.

Write out a personal development plan as a professional expert — including books, courses, training programs, and conferences. Remember, domain expertise is a critical and non-negotiable part of leadership. I build this kind of plan every year and keep notes on every training and book I've gone through.

Start a blog on any platform to practice the skills of persuasion and communicating your ideas to an audience. I've been running a professional blog and a [Telegram channel called "Product Code"](https://t.me/pmgen) for many years now.

Identify your strengths and weaknesses. Helpful tools here include: reflection; feedback from friends and colleagues; and therapy.
