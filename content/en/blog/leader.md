**— Konstantin, why do you think being a leader is worthwhile or interesting?**

— Let's start with what leadership actually is. There are many different definitions. Peter Drucker, for instance, wrote: "Leadership is the ability to help a person go beyond their own limitations."

Leadership is the skill of positively influencing people — one that allows you to achieve ambitious goals through the efforts of every member of a group.

The core responsibilities of a leader, accordingly, are: first, to help each person go beyond their own limitations; and second, to enable different people to work together as productively as possible.

I'd actually reframe the question like this: why is being a leader both interesting and difficult at the same time? Because the path of leadership is, above all, a path of self-improvement and self-discovery. Ichak Adizes identifies several qualities of a leader, and all of them relate to personal character: the ability to act consciously and deliberately; to understand your own strengths and weaknesses; to understand people.

Which brings me to another definition I use:

<div style="font-weight: 200; font-size:1.25em; line-height:1.5;">Leadership is part of personal growth and self-improvement — an endless process of working on yourself and inspiring the people around you.</div>

<br>

**— Can you be a leader within a team or a situation where you're not in charge?**

— A leader and a manager in an organization are not the same thing. A leader inspires, creates a vision of the future, initiates change, and makes decisions that move the organization toward its goals. It's also worth noting that a leader is an expert in a particular domain. A manager, on the other hand, follows job descriptions and optimizes day-to-day operations.

So no, you don't have to be a formal manager or executive to be a leader — though a good leader tends to also be a good manager. Personally, I'm drawn to the role of an informal leader, since it offers more freedom and independence from org structures and corporate politics.

<br>

**— What does it actually look like to "behave like a leader"? For example, how does such a person get results and ask questions?**

— There are many different leadership styles, ranging from authoritarian to laissez-faire (minimal involvement). The communication approach varies depending on which style you use. But broadly speaking, a leader asks open-ended questions to understand someone's perspective and the reasoning behind it. Steering people toward a predetermined answer using SPIN questions or similar techniques isn't really the done thing in tech. You want to hear the actual answer, not a specific one.

When it comes to results, a leader has a very clear vision of the future. They drive outcomes by working with individual motivation and by building an effective team united around a common goal. They also take a mature, hands-off stance — no micromanagement. People don't like being closely monitored or constantly advised, anyway. A leader also doesn't shy away from conflict or try to avoid it — they treat it as a problem to be solved.

<br>

**— How does a leader give feedback?**

— You often see managers and new team leads who handle this really poorly: instead of motivating people and delivering useful feedback, they end up demotivating them. A leader's goal is to make the employee want to adjust their approach and improve their performance on their own. Say someone is falling behind their colleagues and a review is coming up, or it's already clear they won't get a high rating. If the issue is gaps in knowledge, I usually share the tools and techniques that have worked for me — after years in tech, I've tried a lot of things, and I can vouch for what actually works. You won't find this stuff in books.

<br>

**— What else is important to understand about leadership?**

— A leader is proactive and engaged — they don't wait around for a manager to tell them what to do. In today's tech industry, you can't afford to wait anyway. The pay is good, so the expectation is that people bring a high level of self-awareness. The more rigid the hierarchy, the less creative energy, initiative, and motivation you'll find.

At Yandex Practicum, I work with students who come to tech from all kinds of backgrounds, and I see just how differently people approach management and leadership. In one workshop, I gave this scenario: an employee is underperforming, something has clearly happened in their life, but they won't say what — figure it out. Many people who came from traditional industries treated the employee like a resource, an executor who owes the company output. They said so plainly, referenced job responsibilities, and were very formal and directive about it. Those who were already closer to the tech world tended to try to start a friendly conversation, to get the employee to open up and share what was going on. They asked questions, showed care, openness, and empathy. I think it's a very telling example.

**— The big question: where do you start on the path to leadership?**

— With working on yourself. Here are the steps I'd recommend.

Develop self-organization skills and build your own time management system to create a productivity framework that works for you. When I was younger, I studied books by Gleb Arkhangelsky and David Allen on GTD, looking for the tools that would actually work for me specifically.

Write a personal development plan as an expert in your professional field — including books, courses, trainings, and conferences. Remember, being a domain expert is an important and necessary part of leadership. I put together a plan like this every year and keep notes on every training and book I go through.

Start a blog on any platform to practice the skills of persuasion and communicating your perspective to an audience. I've been running a professional blog and the [Telegram channel "Product Code"](https://t.me/pmgen) for many years now.

Identify your strengths and weaknesses. Things that can help: reflection; feedback from friends and colleagues; therapy.
