I came across a fascinating [interview with Andrei Styskin](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%A1%D1%82%D1%8B%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%BD,_%D0%90%D0%BD%D0%B4%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B9_%D0%98%D0%B3%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%87) (Director at Amazon, former CEO of the search division at Yandex), in which he talks about some interesting aspects of Amazon's internal culture — in particular, its "document-centric" approach to meetings and decision-making.

1. Every work meeting is a collaborative exercise where participants work through a pre-prepared document on a specific problem. Part of the time (10 to 20 minutes) is spent reading the document individually; the rest is spent discussing and commenting on it. If open questions remain after the meeting, another meeting is scheduled in the same format. The process continues until requirements are unambiguously defined and agreed upon by all stakeholders.
2. Requirements for any product or feature are developed using the ["Working Backwards"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4VOgUMqcHhU) method — by writing two documents: a "Press Release" and an "FAQ." Long before launch, the product manager writes a press release that articulates a vision of the ideal outcome, giving the entire product team a north star to orient around throughout development.
3. All decisions are made through document reviews, not through verbal conversations in meetings or over a smoke break — as often happens in many workplaces.

Reflecting on my time at Yandex (a great company, though not quite in the same league globally — for context, Yandex's market cap in 2024 was around $10 billion, while Amazon's was $2 trillion), I can say that Yandex's internal culture also encourages employees to express their thinking in writing: detailed wiki documentation is standard practice; task tracker descriptions are written out thoroughly; discussions happen in comments; and internal blogs — both public and anonymous — are common. The more senior the person, the more persuasively and concisely they tend to work with text.

Interestingly, I know several editors and journalists who became highly successful product managers remarkably quickly. I'm convinced that their mastery of written communication played a significant role in that transition. Why else would I be running this Telegram channel?

On the flip side, I often see early-career specialists drowning in a sea of tools — Miro sticky notes, spreadsheets, arrows, Notion features — and producing documents with minimal value or substance. Instead of opening a plain text editor, sitting quietly, organizing their thoughts, and writing them up as a coherent, engaging narrative.

Unfortunately, most company cultures don't nurture writing skills, and not a single management course I'm aware of treats it as something worth developing. So, fellow product managers, this is a skill you'll have to build on your own. The path forward is straightforward: read challenging texts, and write challenging texts.

One important aspect of this kind of culture is reaching shared understanding of a problem and its best solution across all stakeholders. But there's a second aspect — more fundamental and more valuable for the individual thinker: the act of writing allows you to think with sustained focus for hours at a time, far longer than verbal thinking permits. What's more, writing lets you pause, step away, and return later to exactly where you left off — with renewed energy and full attention.

Paradoxically, the final text that emerges from such a focused creative process can differ dramatically from the original idea. Some of the meaning in the finished piece will only come into existence through the deep work of writing it. And some thoughts you'll have to wrestle with — quite literally.

So Amazon's "document-centric" culture does more than bring transparency to communication — it pushes the people building the company's products to develop critical thinking and the ability to solve non-trivial problems. It creates a productive intellectual work environment, and that shows up in the company's competitiveness and business results.

Yes, you can't always influence your company's culture — but developing your own critical thinking skills is within everyone's reach. You don't need expensive training for that. A more useful starting exercise is simply this: pick any topic you find interesting, find a quiet place free from distractions, and write an article about it. It works equally well for professional challenges and personal ones.

Many respected voices in the industry have explored the idea of thinking through writing. I strongly recommend diving into the original materials at the links below.

- [Paul Graham](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%93%D1%80%D1%8D%D0%BC,_%D0%9F%D0%BE%D0%BB) (co-owner of Hacker News, named one of "The 25 Most Influential People on the Web" in 2008) published a post on his blog called ["Putting Ideas into Words"](https://paulgraham.com/words.html), in which he describes the value of essay writing as a tool for thinking through problems.
- [David Heinemeier Hansson](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%A5%D0%B0%D0%BD%D1%81%D1%81%D0%BE%D0%BD,_%D0%94%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B8%D0%B4_%D0%A5%D0%B5%D0%B9%D0%BD%D0%B5%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%B9%D0%B5%D1%80) (co-owner of 37signals, creator of Ruby on Rails) talks about his experience writing and how it shaped his career in a short [YouTube interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ip8jD3Y0FbM). The whole thing can be summed up in one sentence: "Clear writing is clear thinking."
- [Georgy Shchedrovitsky](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%A9%D0%B5%D0%B4%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%86%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B9,_%D0%93%D0%B5%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B3%D0%B8%D0%B9_%D0%9F%D0%B5%D1%82%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%87) (Soviet philosopher and author of numerous academic papers on management) wrote in his book [*Organizational Management Thinking*](https://www.ozon.ru/product/orgupravlencheskoe-myshlenie-shchedrovitskiy-georgiy-petrovich-255067492/): "If a person doesn't write — they don't think." His work has had a profound influence on me as a manager and leader, and I highly recommend it.
- [Anatoly Levenchuk](https://system-school.ru/levenchuk) (founder of the School of Systems Management and author of numerous lectures) published an article ["Cortex and Exocortex"](https://ailev.livejournal.com/1224251.html) arguing that thinking is more productive with a pen and paper — which he associates with the exocortex (an external information-processing system that augments intelligence).
- [Andy Matuschak](https://andymatuschak.org/) (a researcher in UX/UI in the context of learning and knowledge retention) wrote a wonderful piece ["Why books don't work"](https://andymatuschak.org/books/), in which he explores why reading books and listening to lectures alone isn't enough to internalize knowledge. To truly understand material, you need to actively engage with it — and writing essays is one way to do exactly that.
