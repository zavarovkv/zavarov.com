I came across a fascinating [interview with Andrei Styskin](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%A1%D1%82%D1%8B%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%BD,_%D0%90%D0%BD%D0%B4%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B9_%D0%98%D0%B3%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%87) (Director at Amazon, former CEO of the search division at Yandex), in which Andrei talks about some interesting aspects of Amazon's internal culture — specifically, the "document-centric" approach to meetings and decision-making.

1. Every meeting is a collaborative effort in which participants work together on a pre-prepared document focused on a specific problem. Part of the time (10 to 20 minutes) is spent reading the document independently; the rest is used for discussion and commentary. If open questions remain after the meeting, another meeting is scheduled in the same format. The process continues until the requirements are unambiguously defined and agreed upon by all stakeholders.
2. Requirements for any product or feature are developed using the ["Working Backwards"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4VOgUMqcHhU) method, through writing two documents: a "Press Release" and a "FAQ." The product manager writes a press release long before the release date, thereby forming a vision of the ideal launch outcome — a north star that the product team can orient toward throughout development.
3. All decisions are made through document reviews, not through informal verbal conversations in meetings or by the coffee machine, as so often happens in most workplaces.

Reflecting on my time at Yandex (a great company, though not quite global in scale — for comparison, in 2024 Yandex's market cap was $10 billion, while Amazon's was $2 trillion), I can say that the company's internal culture encourages employees to express their thinking in writing: at Yandex, it's standard practice to maintain detailed wiki documentation, write thorough task descriptions in the issue tracker, discuss questions in comments, and keep internal blogs — both public and anonymous. The more senior an employee's position, the more persuasively and concisely they tend to write and communicate their ideas.

Incidentally, I know several editors and journalists who became highly successful product managers remarkably quickly. I'm convinced that their mastery of written language played a significant role in that journey. Otherwise, why would I be running this Telegram channel?

In contrast, I often see junior specialists drowning in a sea of tools — Miro sticky notes, spreadsheets, arrows, Notion features — and producing documents with minimal value or substance. Instead of opening a simple text editor, sitting quietly, and writing out their thoughts as a coherent, logically connected narrative.

Unfortunately, the culture of many companies doesn't foster writing skills, and not a single management course I'm aware of treats this skill as significant. So, fellow product managers, developing this skill is on you. What helps is reading challenging texts — and writing them.

One important dimension of this kind of culture is reaching shared understanding of a problem and its best solution among all stakeholders. The second dimension — more fundamental and more valuable to the individual thinker — is that writing allows us to think in a focused, sustained way, holding our attention on a problem for several hours at a stretch, which is far longer than we can sustain in verbal thought. What's more, thinking through writing lets us pause and step away, then return to exactly where we left off — with restored energy and full attention.

Paradoxically, the substance of a text produced through such a focused creative process can diverge dramatically from the original idea. Some of the meanings in the final piece only emerge through the deep work of writing it. And with some ideas, you'll genuinely have to wrestle.

It turns out that Amazon's "document-centric" culture doesn't just bring transparency to communication — it also encourages the people building the company's products to develop critical thinking and the ability to solve non-trivial problems. The culture creates a productive intellectual work environment, and that shows up in the company's competitiveness and business results.

Yes, it's not always possible to influence the culture of your own company — but developing your own critical thinking skills is within everyone's reach. You don't even need to attend expensive training programs. A more useful starting exercise is simply this: pick any topic that genuinely interests you, find a quiet place free of distractions, and write an article about it. It works equally well for professional challenges and personal ones.

Many interesting people in the industry have written about thinking through writing. I highly recommend exploring the original sources below.

- [Paul Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Graham_(programmer)) (co-owner of Hacker News, named one of "The 25 Most Influential People on the Web" in 2008) published a post on his blog called ["Putting Ideas into Words"](https://paulgraham.com/words.html), in which he describes the value of writing essays as a way of thinking through a problem.
- [David Heinemeier Hansson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Heinemeier_Hansson) (co-owner of 37signals, creator of Ruby on Rails) talks in a short [YouTube interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ip8jD3Y0FbM) about his experience writing articles and the impact it's had on his career. The essence of everything he says can be captured in one line: "Clear writing is clear thinking."
- [Georgy Shchedrovitsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgy_Shchedrovitsky) (Soviet philosopher, author of numerous academic papers on management) wrote in his book [*Organizational Management Thinking*](https://www.ozon.ru/product/orgupravlencheskoe-myshlenie-shchedrovitskiy-georgiy-petrovich-255067492/): "If a person doesn't write, they don't think." Shchedrovitsky's work has had a profound influence on me as a manager and leader — I wholeheartedly recommend exploring it.
- [Anatoly Levenchuk](https://system-school.ru/levenchuk) (founder of the School of Systems Management and author of many lectures) published an article ["Cortex and Exocortex"](https://ailev.livejournal.com/1224251.html) on the idea that thinking is more productive with a pencil and paper — what he associates with the exocortex, an external information-processing system that augments the intellect.
- [Andy Matuschak](https://andymatuschak.org/) (a researcher in UX/UI in the context of learning and knowledge retention) wrote a wonderful piece called ["Why books don't work"](https://andymatuschak.org/books/), in which he reflects on and explains why simply reading books or listening to lectures isn't enough to absorb knowledge. To truly internalize material, you need to actively engage with it — and writing essays is one way to do exactly that.

And finally, a note to myself: <span class="underline">write down your thoughts and ideas more often — work through them in writing.</span>
