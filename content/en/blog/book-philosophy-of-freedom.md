“Freedom” has many meanings. Lars Svendsen suggests it may be the most ambiguous concept of all. I deliberately wrote “most” and “of all” to emphasize just how broad that claim is. Two people picked at random will understand freedom differently, drawing on their own experience and values.

Svendsen **reflects** on the ontology, politics and ethics of freedom. I want to emphasize “reflects”: reading the book, you feel his systematic, painstaking determination to get to the truth.

## The ontology of freedom

Determinism assumes that any future event can be predicted: things and events in the world are connected through cause and effect. According to this theory, the state of everything in the universe at time t follows from its state at the previous moment. Broadly speaking, complete knowledge of the universe at time t would allow us to predict any event at time t + n, where n → ∞.

Indeterminism suggests otherwise: one outcome may be more likely than another, but the second remains possible.

The author examines human freedom from the following positions:

- Hard determinism: human beings are determined and not free.
- Libertarianism: human beings are not determined and are free.
- Compatibilism: human beings are determined and free.
- Skepticism: human beings are neither determined nor free.

Svendsen leans toward indeterminism, while noting that there are no sufficiently convincing arguments either for or against determinism. Intuitively, he is inclined toward libertarianism, but he does not impose his view on the reader.

A takeaway on autonomy: “People become increasingly free and increasingly responsible for their actions as they come to understand, in every sense, what they are doing, and as their actions increasingly correspond to a definite, clearly expressed intention.” Working on ourselves gradually increases our freedom and self-determination.

## The politics of freedom

Comparing rankings of economic freedom with the UN's Human Development Index reveals many parallels. The book provides sources. People in countries with greater economic freedom enjoy higher living standards, better education and fewer health problems, with some exceptions, such as the United States.

Countries with greater economic freedom also tend to have less economic inequality.

## On negative freedom

Negative freedom concerns possible actions rather than actual ones. Positive freedom concerns autonomy.

Hobbes's definition of a free person: “A free person is someone who is not prevented from doing what they wish, provided they have the physical and mental ability to do it.”

Hobbes's account of negative freedom: “The purpose of laws, which are simply rules established by the sovereign, is not to keep people from all voluntary actions. It is to direct their movements so that they do not harm themselves through unrestrained desires, recklessness and carelessness—just as fences are erected not to stop travelers, but to keep them from losing their way.”

Isaiah Berlin's account of freedom: “The positive meaning of Freedom comes from the desire to be my own master. I want my life and decisions to depend on myself rather than on external forces. I want to be an agent, not to submit to another person's will. I want to be a subject rather than an object: to follow my own reasons and conscious purposes instead of acting under outside influences. I want to be someone who makes decisions and chooses a course of action, rather than obeying nature or other people as though I were a thing, an animal or a slave incapable of living as a human being—that is, incapable of defining and carrying out my own purposes and strategy. This is at least part of what I mean when I say that I am rational and that reason distinguishes me from the rest of the world. Above all, I want to regard myself as a thinking, willing, active being who bears responsibility for my choices and can explain them by reference to my own ideas and purposes. To the extent that I believe this is true, I feel free, and vice versa.”

Taylor's account of freedom: “You are not free if you are driven by a motivation—fear, imposed standards or false consciousness—that conflicts with your self-realization.”

For a while, Svendsen turns to political and religious topics that are less close to my interests, so I am leaving out many of those details.

Some books and other works Svendsen references throughout the book, useful as further reading:

- Walden Two, B. F. Skinner.
- Erewhon, Samuel Butler.
- Politics, Aristotle.
- The Spirit of the Laws, Montesquieu.
- Abraham Lincoln's speech on freedom and slavery.
- The Dice Man, Luke Rhinehart.
- Francis Fukuyama's essay on the end of history.
- Phenomenology of Spirit, G. W. F. Hegel.
- The Origins of Political Order, Francis Fukuyama, his latest book at the time.
- “The Future of History,” an article by Francis Fukuyama.
- The Elementary Particles, Michel Houellebecq.
- “Harrison Bergeron,” Kurt Vonnegut.
