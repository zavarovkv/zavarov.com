The goal of strategy is to move away from "build whatever anyone asks for" toward a deliberate position aimed at creating a better future for both users and the business.

As tempting as it would be, there is no universal product strategy template — no template at all, really. The format depends on the maturity of the company and its people, team structure, business model, business strategy, and a whole host of other factors.

That's exactly why there are so many definitions of product strategy, each more abstract than the last. Authors of well-known books lay out elegant principles, but there's a chasm between those principles and the actual work. Take "Good Strategy, Bad Strategy" — the ideas sound convincing, but try grounding them in a real product process. If you've managed to do it, reach out — I'd love to discuss.

Read any article on building product strategy — even ones written by leaders from Avito (I mention them because of their genuinely well-developed product processes) — and you'll find the same recurring comment: "Great article. Really enjoyed reading it, but it feels like a horse in a vacuum."

Product strategy is an ongoing working process. Depending on the stage of the product and the context, strategy can take very different forms.

Here are some perfectly workable examples of how it can show up.

1/ Strategy as a person. A leader who accumulates knowledge across business, marketing, finance, and product — and translates it to the team through decisions and priorities. This works, but "strategy living inside someone's head" stops scaling as the team grows.

2/ Or "strategy as a manifesto" — an internal team artifact containing a set of principles and answers to "what do we do" and "why." This kind of strategy can be written in an evening, but it can lose touch with reality just as quickly.

Strategy is not a templated document you write once. It's a system that lives and evolves alongside the product.
