The goal of strategy is to move from "we build whatever anyone asks for" to a considered position aimed at creating a better future for both users and the business.

As much as we'd like one to exist, there is no universal product strategy template — no template at all, really. The format depends on the company's maturity, the team's experience, organizational structure, business model, business strategy, and a huge number of other factors.

That's precisely why there are so many definitions of product strategy — each more abstract than the last. Authors of well-known books lay out elegant principles, but there's a chasm between those principles and the actual work. Take "Good Strategy, Bad Strategy" — the ideas sound compelling, but try grounding them in a real product process. If you've managed it, let me know — I'd love to discuss.

Read any article about building a product strategy, even from leaders at Avito (I use them as an example because of their exceptionally well-developed product processes), and you'll find a common reaction: "Great article. Really enjoyed reading it — but it feels a bit like a horse in a vacuum."

Product strategy is an ongoing working process. At different stages of a product's life and in different contexts, strategy can take very different forms.

Here are some genuinely workable examples.

1/ Strategy as a specific person. A leader who accumulates knowledge across business, marketing, finance, and product — and translates it to the team through decisions and priorities. This works, but "strategy living in someone's head" stops scaling as the team grows.

2/ Or "strategy as a manifesto" — an internal team artifact containing a set of principles and answers to the questions "what do we do" and "why." This kind of strategy can be written in an evening, but it can lose touch with reality just as quickly.

Strategy is not a templated document you write once. It's a system that lives and evolves alongside the product.
