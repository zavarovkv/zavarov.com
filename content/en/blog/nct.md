I came across an interesting approach to goal-setting — NCT (Narratives, Commitments, Tasks) by Ravi Mehta [1]. What makes it compelling is that it positions itself as an alternative to OKR and could be genuinely useful for many product teams. Before diving into NCT, let's take a quick look at the history of goal-setting frameworks.

In 1954, Peter Drucker introduced the concept of MBO (Management by Objectives) in his book *The Practice of Management* [2]. The core idea is to set goals at the company level and cascade them down to individual contributors at every level. For example, many services within Yandex still use a similar approach today — there's even a dedicated internal tool for cascading goals called "Golzyatnitsa."

In 1981, George Doran introduced the SMART goal-setting framework in a [journal article](https://community.mis.temple.edu/mis0855002fall2015/files/2015/10/S.M.A.R.T-Way-Management-Review.pdf). Before SMART gained widespread adoption, I imagine goals were described something like: *Person X must complete the following list of tasks by a specific date.*

In 1983, Andrew Grove (co-founder of Intel) developed the OKR methodology — Objectives and Key Results — and used it to improve Intel's operational processes. Grove is a legendary figure in management culture and authored several must-read books, including *High Output Management* ([available in Russian translation](https://www.livelib.ru/book/1000578066-vysokoeffektivnyj-menedzhment-endryu-grouv)), where he first introduced OKR.

In 1999, Andrew Grove brought OKR to Google.

In 2017, [Felipe Castro](https://www.linkedin.com/in/meetfelipe/) helped spread OKR beyond Google and has since become one of the framework's leading voices — consulting, speaking, and writing books on the subject. One of his books, [*The Beginner's Guide to OKR*](https://scrumtrek.ru/blog/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/the-beginners-guide-to-okr-scrumtrek-ru.pdf), is available in a Russian translation by Scrumtrek.

So, every manager has heard of OKR — but in my experience, it's not that commonly adopted in product-focused tech companies (though there are successful examples, of course). The transition from traditional goal-setting to OKR comes with a significant cultural shift that isn't easy to navigate — and may not always be worth it.

I've tried implementing OKR across several teams and ran into a number of challenges, including some that Ravi Mehta himself describes:

1. OKR insists: "No number, no key result." But in real life, it's perfectly normal for a project to still be in development at the end of a quarter — meaning the work done doesn't yet show up in product or business metrics. My workaround was to use a traditional goal-setting approach for products in the launch phase, and OKR for products in the growth phase. Not exactly convenient when you're managing a portfolio of products at different lifecycle stages.
2. OKR doesn't help managers and teams forecast or build a roadmap for new feature releases — without which it's hard to keep the team focused or manage stakeholder expectations.

In his NCT framework, Ravi Mehta proposes the following refinements to OKR [3]:

- Add more context to the Objective in the form of a **Narrative** that explains why the goal matters and how it connects to the broader strategy.
- Make Key Results binding commitments. Ravi Mehta reframes them as **Commitments**, which can be defined either in a quantitative format (as OKR prescribes) or in a project-delivery format (Launch-It).
- For each commitment, work with the team to develop an execution plan and build a **roadmap of Tasks**. Unlike Commitments, the plan and tasks can be adjusted as things evolve.

I don't think Ravi Mehta reinvented the wheel here — he mostly formalized an approach that many teams already use intuitively. And one that tends to stick far better than OKR. Still, it's worth knowing about.
