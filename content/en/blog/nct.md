I came across an interesting approach to goal-setting — NCT (Narratives, Commitments, Tasks) by Ravi Mehta [1]. What makes it compelling is that it positions itself as an alternative to OKR and could be genuinely useful for many product teams. Before diving into NCT, let's take a quick look at the history of goal-setting frameworks.

In 1954, Peter Drucker introduced the concept of MBO (Management by Objectives) in his book *The Practice of Management* [2]. The core idea: set goals at the company level and cascade them down to individual contributors at every level. Many services within Yandex, for instance, still use a similar approach today. They even have a dedicated internal tool for cascading goals called "Golzyatnitsa" (roughly, "the goal keeper").

In 1981, George Doran formalized the concept of SMART goals and published it in a [journal article](https://community.mis.temple.edu/mis0855002fall2015/files/2015/10/S.M.A.R.T-Way-Management-Review.pdf). My guess is that before SMART gained traction, goals were written something like: *Person X must complete a list of tasks by a specific date.*

In 1983, Andrew Grove (co-founder of Intel) developed the OKR methodology — a system for setting objectives and measuring results — and used it to improve Intel's operational processes. Grove is something of a legend in management culture and authored several books worth reading, including *High Output Management* ([available in Russian translation](https://www.livelib.ru/book/1000578066-vysokoeffektivnyj-menedzhment-endryu-grouv)), where he first introduced the concept of OKRs.

In 1999, Andrew Grove helped introduce OKR methodology at Google.

In 2017, [Felipe Castro](https://www.linkedin.com/in/meetfelipe/) brought OKRs to a wider audience beyond Google, and has since become one of the foremost OKR evangelists — consulting, speaking, and writing books on the subject. One of his books, [*The Beginner's Guide to OKR*](https://scrumtrek.ru/blog/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/the-beginners-guide-to-okr-scrumtrek-ru.pdf), is available in Russian translation from Scrumtrek.

Most managers have heard of OKRs, but in my experience, they're not all that common in product-driven tech companies (though successful examples do exist). The shift from traditional goal-setting to OKRs requires a massive cultural leap — one that's hard to pull off, and arguably not always worth attempting.

I've tried implementing OKRs across several teams and ran into a number of challenges — including the ones Ravi Mehta writes about:

1. OKR doctrine insists: "No number, no key result." But in practice, it's perfectly normal for a project to still be in development at the end of a quarter, meaning the work done has no measurable impact on product or business metrics yet. My workaround: use a traditional goal-setting approach for products in the launch phase, and OKRs for products in the growth phase. Not exactly convenient when you're managing a portfolio of products at different lifecycle stages.
2. OKRs don't help a manager or team forecast and build a roadmap for shipping new features. Without a roadmap, it's hard to keep the team focused and manage stakeholder expectations.

In his NCT framework, Ravi Mehta proposes the following improvements to OKR [3]:

- Add more context to the Objective in the form of a **Narrative** — an explanation of why the goal matters and how it connects to strategy.
- Make Key Results mandatory commitments. Mehta calls them **Commitments**, and they can be framed either quantitatively (as in standard OKR) or as project-based milestones ("Launch-It" format).
- For each commitment, work with the team to develop an implementation plan and build out a **Task** roadmap. Unlike Commitments, these plans and tasks can be adjusted as things evolve.

I don't think Ravi Mehta invented the wheel here — he's largely formalized an approach that many teams already use intuitively. And for those teams, it tends to stick far better than OKR. Worth knowing about.
