Looking at the top edTech companies in the Russian-speaking internet market, I noticed an interesting scaling problem baked into the economics of their courses — one they've simply learned to live with. I'm not talking about product-led edTech platforms like Lingualeo, but companies that create educational courses for upskilling or career transitions.

<h2>Product EdTech Theorem #1</h2>

<q>The absence of deadlines in an educational course leads to ↑ Revenue and ↓ Completion Rate (COR). Conversely, having deadlines leads to ↓ Revenue and ↑ Completion Rate.</q>

This simple theorem is easy to derive intuitively and is confirmed by managers at edTech companies. The practical takeaway for prospective students: in a self-paced learning format, you're less likely to finish the course and earn a certificate — and less likely to get your money back.

A corollary follows from this Theorem:

<q>In educational courses without deadlines, the number of refunds → 0.</q>

<h2>Type "A" Companies</h2>

These companies build courses with a noble goal: to help as many students as possible reach their educational objectives — typically, landing a job.

The proven way to boost Completion Rate (COR) is through strong motivation via deadlines and a high-stakes, "pass or fail" structure. This is exactly what Product EdTech Theorem #1 says. But the concept of "hard deadlines" is impossible without a team of experts who coordinate students, answer questions quickly, and review assignments.

A strong student experience shows up in high NPS scores (above 50%), which in turn drives a large number of new paying students through organic and referral channels — and generates strong Revenue (Revenue = avg. price × students). But it also drives up Costs as the expert team grows.

The result: as the student count grows, Revenue grows linearly — and so do variable Costs. This doesn't fit the economics of a product business, where revenue scales exponentially while costs remain fixed (or capped).

<h2>Type "B" Companies</h2>

These companies treat courses as a product business: minimize variable costs and grow revenue by attracting as many students as possible through any means available.

"Minimize costs" unravels in the opposite direction: no student support team → no hard deadlines → a minimal share of students actually finish the course → low NPS (zero percent at best) → very few new paying students coming in organically.

The result: they set out to build a product business and even managed to drive variable costs to zero — but they critically lack the student volume needed to boost revenue.

<h2>The Dilemma</h2>

It comes down to choosing between path "A" and path "B." Neither allows you to build a true product business around a single educational course. So companies find their optimal growth strategy in launching as many courses as possible across every direction imaginable — and scaling through sheer course volume.

If both paths lead to the same strategy of scaling courses, and the order of magnitude of profit is roughly the same either way, then the question becomes: why do so many companies choose path "B"? Especially when path "A" guarantees a strong market reputation, while path "B" means a severely limited addressable market and rising Customer Acquisition Costs (CAC).
