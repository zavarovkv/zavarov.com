While comparing the top edTech companies in the Russian-speaking internet market, I noticed a funny scaling problem baked into their course economics — one they've all learned to live with. I'm not talking about product-driven edTech platforms like Lingualeo, but companies that create educational courses for upskilling or career transitions.

<h2>Product EdTech Theorem #1</h2>

<q>The absence of deadlines in an educational course leads to ↑ Revenue and ↓ Completion Rate (COR). Conversely, having deadlines leads to ↓ Revenue and ↑ Completion Rate.</q>

This is a simple theorem — easy to derive intuitively and confirmed by managers across edTech companies. The practical takeaway for prospective students: in self-paced learning, the probability of finishing the course, earning a certificate, or getting your money back is significantly lower.

And a corollary to the Theorem:

<q>In educational courses without deadlines, the number of refunds → 0.</q>

<h2>Type "A" Companies</h2>

These companies build courses with a genuine goal: to help as many students as possible achieve their educational objectives — most often, landing a job.

The proven way to boost Completion Rate (COR) is through strong motivation: hard deadlines and an elimination-style structure. That's exactly what Product EdTech Theorem #1 tells us. But the "hard deadlines" model only works if you have a team of experts to coordinate students, answer questions quickly, and review assignments.

When students have a great learning experience, NPS scores reflect it — often above 50%. That leads to a steady stream of new paying students through organic growth and referrals, and drives Revenue up (Revenue = avg. price × students). But it also drives up Costs, as the expert team grows alongside the student base.

The result: as the number of students scales, both Revenue and variable Costs grow linearly. That's not the economics of a product business — where Revenue grows exponentially while Costs stay fixed (or at least capped).

<h2>Type "B" Companies</h2>

These companies treat courses as a product business: minimize variable costs and grow revenue by attracting as many students as possible through any means available.

"Minimize costs" plays out as a chain reaction in reverse: no student support team → no hard deadlines → minimal course completion → low NPS (0% on a good day) → very few new paying students coming in organically.

In the end, they set out to build a product business and did succeed in zeroing out variable costs — but they're critically short on students to actually drive revenue growth.

<h2>The Dilemma</h2>

The dilemma is choosing between path "A" and path "B." Neither allows you to build a true product business from a single course. So companies find their optimal growth path by launching as many courses as possible, across as many directions as they can — and scaling through sheer course volume.

If both paths lead to the same answer (scale the number of courses) and deliver roughly the same order of magnitude in profit, the question becomes: why do so many companies choose path "B"? Especially when path "A" guarantees a strong market reputation, while path "B" results in a severely limited addressable market and an ever-rising CAC.
