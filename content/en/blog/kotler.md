And a product manager is a marketing specialist.

This definition is obvious to people who studied marketing in college. But it's not always clear to those who came to product management from a technical background.

As someone who came up through the technical ranks, I long believed that "marketing" was limited to finding promotion channels, communicating a product's value to users, and selling goods (physical or digital — didn't matter). But that understanding of marketing is very, very far from the truth.

Though it's hard to argue that these are exactly the kinds of tasks handled by specialists at digital companies — people often called Marketing Managers (MM) or even Product Marketing Managers (PMM).

The funny thing is that half a century ago, the renowned Philip Kotler was already writing about the confusion between "marketing" and "sales and advertising." Here's what he writes in his book *Principles of Marketing*:

<q>What does the term "marketing" actually mean? Most people mistakenly equate marketing with sales and advertising. Many are surprised to learn that selling is not the most important part of marketing at all. Selling is just the tip of the marketing iceberg — and not even its most significant part. If the marketer does a good job identifying consumer needs, developing the right products, pricing them appropriately, and establishing a proper distribution system, those products will sell themselves with ease.</q>

And a second passage:

<q>The core activities of marketing consist of: product development; research; establishing communication; pricing; and providing service to users.</q>

So, fellow product managers — in Kotler's terms, we are the "marketing specialists," while our colleagues in marketing are the "sales (promotion) specialists." Just don't tell them that, or they might get upset and take it personally — and then you'll have to figure out how to promote your own product yourself.
