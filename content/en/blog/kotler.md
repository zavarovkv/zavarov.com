And a product manager is a marketing specialist.

This definition is obvious to people who studied marketing in college. But it's not always clear to those who came to product management from a technical background.

As someone who came up through the technical side, I long believed that "marketing" was limited to finding promotion channels, communicating product value to users, and selling goods — physical or digital, it didn't matter. But that understanding of marketing is very, very far from the truth.

That said, it's hard to argue that these are precisely the kinds of tasks handled by specialists at digital companies who are often called marketers (Marketing Manager — MM) or even product marketers (Product Marketing Manager — PMM).

The funny thing is that half a century ago, the legendary Philip Kotler was already writing about the confusion between "marketing" and "sales and advertising." Here's what he wrote in his book *Principles of Marketing*:

<q>What does the term "marketing" actually mean? Most people mistakenly equate marketing with selling and advertising. Many are surprised to learn that selling is not the most important part of marketing. Selling is only the tip of the marketing iceberg, and not the most significant part at that. If the marketer has done a good job in the key areas of marketing — identifying consumer needs, developing suitable products, pricing them appropriately, and establishing a distribution system — those products will sell themselves.</q>

And a second quote:

<q>The core activities of marketing include product development, research, communication, pricing, and customer service.</q>

So, fellow product managers — in Kotler's terminology, we are the "marketing specialists," while our colleagues in marketing are the "sales and promotion specialists." Just don't tell them that, though, or they might get upset and take it personally — and then you'll be left promoting your own product all by yourself.
