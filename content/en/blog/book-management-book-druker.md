Drucker is worth rereading from time to time. After reading a second book of his, I understood why some major global companies make his work required reading for new employees.

I often like to argue about how management should be organized. Some friends also enjoy criticizing the management of the company where I work. But how do you measure management effectiveness? By the way internal processes are organized? No. By the methodologies used? Not that either. I cannot see a more balanced or rational way to judge it than by the company's results and the measures of success it has achieved.

It is amusing to hear people criticize processes at companies that have reached great heights and continue to advance. The belief that there must be one correct organizational structure rests on a misconception.

Drucker helps you step back and look at management beyond business management: interactions within a team, managing yourself, and making both yourself and your team more effective.

Back to Drucker and the book:

> Each employee must be able to work in several organizational structures at once. They perform one assignment as a team member and another, in parallel, as part of a management or supervisory body. A person who is the boss in their own unit may act as a partner in an alliance, association, and so on. Organizational structures must become part of management's toolkit.

A few words about what a team actually is. You do not often encounter a genuinely well-built, excellent team at work, yet that is what underpins a project's success.

> …all these people consider themselves a team, and everyone in the hospital sees them that way. They really are a team: every member instantly adjusts their actions to the slightest change in the rhythm or progress of an operation, without special orders or even hints from colleagues.

On managing people and working with a team, Maslow demonstrated convincingly that different people require different approaches and management styles. One of Douglas McGregor's most widely quoted books argues that managers must choose between two—and only two!—ways of managing people, supported by Theory X and Theory Y, of which only Theory Y is correct. I look at competitors and at companies at a similar stage of development and see different management styles everywhere. Different patterns, different processes; sometimes processes are not defined at all, leaving managers room for a creative, considered and productive approach.

Another central point from Drucker: do not get stuck on models developed a hundred years ago, when large-scale knowledge work did not yet exist.

Many people will find it obvious that someone with a wealth of knowledge is less a subordinate than an associate. For a good specialist, knowledge is a means of production. Good specialists are mobile and can leave at any time. They need to know the organization's mission and believe in it. They need ongoing professional development. They need to see results.

It follows that employees are partners, and partnership rules out command and assumes equality among the participants.

**People do not need to be managed.**

**The task is to lead people.**

**The aim is to make each individual's particular skills and knowledge as productive as possible.**

Management is the specific tool, function and apparatus that enables an organization to achieve the results it needs.

> The most difficult, expensive and pointless activity is fighting to keep a corpse from decomposing.

## On the future and planning

The only policy that can ensure success is consciously building the future. Taking on that task is risky. Not doing so is worse. Many will fail, but some will succeed. Among those who never try, there will certainly be no winners.

## Knowledge work is our present and our future

The most valuable asset of any twenty-first-century organization, commercial or nonprofit, will be its knowledge workers and their productivity. Six factors determine a knowledge worker's productivity:

1. It requires a clear answer to the question: what is the task?
2. Knowledge workers must manage themselves.
3. Continuous innovation must be an integral part of knowledge work.
4. Knowledge workers must continually learn and continually teach.
5. Their productivity is measured by quality.
6. To improve their productivity, they must be seen as capital rather than a cost. They need to want to work for the organization and prefer it to the alternatives.

Improving the productivity of knowledge work is management's most important task in the twenty-first century.
