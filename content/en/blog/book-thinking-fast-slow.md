Daniel Kahneman was a psychologist who was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002.

A book the entire digital community seemed to have read a couple of years earlier. It examines the mistakes of intuitive thinking in detail and repeatedly stresses how much luck contributes to most success stories. Kahneman supports his ideas with many experiments and examples.

He distinguishes between fast thinking (System 1) and slow thinking (System 2). The terms “System 1” and “System 2,” introduced by psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West, are widely used in psychology.

## Fast thinking

Some features of fast thinking, or System 1:

1. It is automatic and does not give a sense of deliberate control.
2. Its main function is to monitor and update your personal model of the world: your sense of what is normal.
3. It is not consciously accessible.
4. Memory belongs to System 1.
5. It supplies information to System 2.
6. It is impulsive and intuitive.
7. It produces fast, often accurate intuitive judgments, but also many systematic errors in our assumptions.
8. Mood affects it: when we are upset or uncomfortable, we lose touch with our intuition.

An example of fast thinking:

- Identifying which object is closer.

## Slow thinking

Some features of slow thinking, or System 2:

1. It requires purposeful, effortful and orderly work.
2. It is characterized by the limits of deliberate checking and search.
3. Demanding tasks require self-control, and exercising it is unpleasant and exhausting. After using self-control on one task, you are reluctant to use it on another, even if you could do so when necessary. The effects of ego depletion can be counteracted by consuming glucose.
4. It can reason and is cautious, but in some people it is also lazy.
5. It believes it is in charge and knows the reasons for its choices, but this is not true.
6. System 1 supplies a feeling of familiarity; System 2 uses that feeling to decide whether a statement is true.
7. A good mood weakens System 2's control. People in a good mood have better intuition and greater creativity, but are less vigilant and more prone to logical errors.
8. It actively searches memory, performs difficult calculations, makes comparisons, plans and chooses.

An example of slow thinking:

- Counting the occurrences of the letter “a” in a text.

## The Cognitive Reflection Test

This test was proposed by Shane Frederick. On the Russian-language internet, it is known as “the shortest IQ test”:

1. A tennis racket and a tennis ball cost $1.10 together. The racket costs $1 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?
2. Five machines at a textile factory take five minutes to make five items. How many minutes would 100 machines take to make 100 items?
3. Water lilies grow in a pond. Every day their number doubles. If they take 48 days to cover the whole pond, how many days would they take to cover half of it?

The correct answers are 5 cents, 5 minutes and 47 days.

## Regression to the mean

> “Rewarding improved performance works better than punishing mistakes. This proposition has been confirmed repeatedly in studies of pigeons, rats, other animals and human beings.”

Talent and luck:

- Success = talent + luck.
- Great success = a little more talent + a lot of luck.

## Intuition

Kahneman places intuitive decisions within fast thinking. In general, it is worth checking them with slow thinking. At the same time, he believes professional intuition can be developed under the following conditions:

1. A stable, predictable environment.
2. Extensive practice within that stable environment.
3. Good, timely feedback.

## Some terms

The affect heuristic: making decisions and judgments on the basis of immediate feelings of liking or dislike, with little deliberation or argument.

The halo effect: a tendency to see everything about a person positively or negatively, including things you have not observed. For example:

- The halo effect is so powerful that we resist the idea that the same actions can be right or wrong depending on the situation, or that the same person can be flexible and rigid. It reverses cause and effect: we believe a company is struggling because its leaders are inflexible, when the leaders may only appear inflexible because the company is in decline. This is how illusions of understanding arise.

Anchoring: the same house appears more attractive when its asking price is higher, even if you are determined not to be influenced by it.

The conjunction fallacy: judging two events occurring together to be more likely than one of those events alone. For example:

- People often find a more detailed scenario more likely, against the rules of logic. This is a trap for forecasters and their clients: scenarios with extra details are more convincing, but less likely to come true.

WYSIATI: What You See Is All There Is.

Prospect theory rests on the existence of a reference point and the observation that losses feel larger than corresponding gains. An interesting example:

- Poor people are another group in whom the endowment effect is hard to expect. In prospect theory, poverty means living below your reference point. There are things a person needs but cannot obtain, leaving them permanently “in the red.” Small amounts of money they receive are therefore perceived as reducing a loss rather than producing a gain.

The sunk-cost fallacy: investing more in a losing position despite better opportunities elsewhere. This costly mistake appears in decisions large and small. For example:

- Driving through a blizzard because you have already paid for a ticket is a sunk-cost fallacy.

## Some quotations

> “Attention training improved not only executive control but also performance on nonverbal intelligence tests, and that improvement lasted for several months.”

> “A rational person is an engaged person. Lazy thinking is a flaw of the reflective mind, a failure of rationality.”

> “Thinking about money encourages individualism: a reluctance to interact with others, depend on them, or accept their requests.”

> “If you want to appear intelligent and trustworthy, do not use complicated words where simple ones will do. Dressing familiar ideas in pretentious language is taken as a sign of low intelligence and unreliable information.”

> “There is evidence that empty but persuasive messages, such as advertising, have more influence on people who are tired and depleted.”

> “People's reluctance to infer particular cases from general facts is matched only by their readiness to infer general facts from particular cases.”

> “Winners are not judged: leaders who were lucky avoid punishment for excessively risky actions. Instead, they are seen as unusually talented and perceptive because they succeeded, while their prudent critics are dismissed as cowardly mediocrities. A few accidental victories can give a reckless executive or a desperate military leader an aura of foresight and courage.”

> “That is the problem with neglecting duration. You give good and bad memories equal weight even when the good period lasted ten times longer than the bad one.”
