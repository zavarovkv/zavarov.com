These "deadly sins" can serve as a self-assessment checklist for both junior and more experienced managers.

So, here are the mistakes — and how to fix them.

<h2>1. Believing in Multitasking</h2>

Every product manager faces enormous flows of information — countless initiatives and product development ideas that no single person can possibly process within the time available. Stakeholders push their agendas, users complain, the calendar is packed with meetings, and the Product Backlog grows by the day.

Instead of focusing on the tasks that truly matter for the product, a junior manager scatters their time and energy across a wide range of goals. The core mistake is failing to understand that attention is a limited resource — there simply isn't enough of it to handle everything. This resource is consumed both when we're actively working toward a goal and when we're just keeping a task alive in our already-overloaded working memory.

It's worth remembering that "attention is a significantly limited process. When additional tasks arise, it gets depleted — and if those tasks are complex, it gets depleted fast" [9]. And time management comes down to two simple principles:

1. Start by writing down every unfinished task you have [1].
2. Every thought spinning around in your head burns "mental fuel" (a great term coined by Maxim Dorofeev). "If we can build habits that let us write things down instead of trying to remember them, we'll be able to live not just more productively, but more calmly" [2].

And the old saying about chasing two rabbits and catching neither makes exactly the same point.

**How to fix it?**

1. Ruthlessly eliminate multitasking [11].
2. Accept the reality of the job: it's normal for stakeholders to want everything at once; it's normal to have a Product Backlog stretching a year or more ahead; it's normal to focus on what matters and not spend your attention on things that don't.
3. Build the habit of writing down all ideas and tasks — in text or visual form. The goal is to avoid losing information, avoid overloading your working memory, and stop wasting your attention on things that can be captured elsewhere.
4. Focus on the product strategy if one exists; if it doesn't (which happens) — align on the key priorities for the coming quarter with your product lead. For requests that fall outside the product strategy, learn to say "no" politely.
5. Keep in mind that effective work requires energy and a good state of mind, which depend directly on quality sleep, nutrition, physical activity, adequate intake of vitamins, and rest in all its forms.
6. Remember what might seem obvious: if you take the time to learn time management tools and pick a few that work for you — say, the Pomodoro Technique or maintaining a daily to-do list — your productivity will improve significantly.

<h2>2. Fear of Making Decisions</h2>

A product manager is the driving force behind a product's development, and experience, decisiveness, courage, and ambition largely determine a product's success in the market. Yet junior managers are often afraid to make even decisions that have virtually no direct impact on product strategy.

Hesitancy, even in its mildest form, affects the speed and quality of product development. And if a product manager is afraid to make decisions even in serious situations — or keeps delaying them or passing responsibility to others — this will inevitably lead to project failure and career derailment [3].

Indecisiveness often hides behind the guise of running another product experiment or testing another hypothesis. Instead of moving fast and putting the product in front of users, there's an endless cycle of research conducted under the banner of "product hypotheses."

Product management requires certain qualities and knowledge that are acquired over time. But one of the key ingredients of a great PM is character — the ability to tackle problems head-on, make mistakes early on, and gain the necessary experience through doing.

A PM should be able to analyze their own strengths and weaknesses and work on improving the latter rather than burying the problem. For example, if someone is afraid of public speaking or struggles to hold their own at a negotiating table, they need to overcome that fear through training and self-work — and perhaps through therapy.

It's important to remember that experience is gained through trial and error, and after making an unsuccessful decision, you'll face another important choice. And there's nothing wrong with being wrong again — it's all part of accumulating knowledge, the very principle of quantity turning into quality, and growing your professional craft.

**How to fix it?**

1. Keep the outcomes you're aiming for — and the primary business goal — constantly in mind. I've seen it happen many times, and fallen into the trap myself: in the middle of a creative problem-solving process, you lose sight of the original goal, and the solution you arrive at is "brilliant" — but doesn't actually move you toward where you need to go.
2. Different decisions have different levels of consequences, and often "we can correct a mistake in a matter of hours. In other cases, the consequences can put the product — or the entire business — at risk" [4]. High-stakes decisions are best made in the morning, when your reserves of "mental fuel" or "psychic energy" [5] are at their highest.

<h2>3. Making Decisions Alone</h2>

The opposite extreme is making important product decisions unilaterally, without consulting colleagues or leadership. I've seen it more than once: a product manager makes a product decision without discussing it with the stakeholders involved, and only after the release do the flaws surface — flaws that then have to be patched up in a hurry.

**How to fix it?**

1. Don't be embarrassed to discuss problems and solutions with colleagues. Set your professional pride aside. Good advice can only help you, and there's nothing wrong with turning to people who have experience in these areas and who've been in situations similar to yours. And those colleagues aren't just managers — often, they're people in operational roles who have more hands-on experience with these issues than your boss does [6]. Moreover, when decisions are made in close collaboration with the team, and disagreements are resolved through testing and discussion, a product manager signals to the team that their input matters — which makes for a healthier work environment [4].
2. Keep in mind that help has its limits. Don't offload the responsibility for decisions onto colleagues or delegate your own authority to someone else. "Unnecessary help is harmful — that is, if I ask someone for help to deal with circumstances I should handle myself, I seriously undermine my belief in my own abilities." [3] That's reason enough to think about balance — accepting help while staying independent in your work.

<h2>4. Not Going All In</h2>

It's important for a junior product manager to be curious, to eagerly absorb everything new, and to enthusiastically build skills — not just in product management, but in general management as well.

That said, it's worth remembering that what's taught even in the best courses is by no means a guaranteed path to success. Knowledge gained through training can actually become a trap for junior specialists in general, and product managers in particular — because what you're taught is often fundamentally different from what you'll actually do on the job.

I've met many people who were sold a romanticized version of product management on PM courses, but who are completely unprepared for the day-to-day work. They stand in sharp contrast to those who figure things out on their own and work on their products with genuine enthusiasm and fire in their eyes.

So ask yourself: "Is this really something I want to be doing right now — with 200% engagement — or not?" If not — don't waste your time; find what actually inspires you.

And if the answer is yes, remember: "Your work should have three qualities: it's something you have a natural aptitude for, something you find deeply interesting, and something that gives you the opportunity to do excellent work" [7].

**How to fix it?**

1. Alexander Gorny once described in an interview how working 80-hour weeks at Mail.Ru Group helped him build his career and learn to find genuine satisfaction in his professional life. He even recommended that every junior specialist try working at that pace for at least a year. It's the honest truth, so:
2. Set yourself an ambitious, bold professional goal — one that motivates you to be a relentless machine in pursuing it (relentless toward yourself, first and foremost). The goal can be anything: getting promoted, making your product a market leader, becoming a head of product. Working 80-hour weeks without a goal is hard labor; with a goal, it's just the daily grind on the way to something that matters.
3. Recognize that there are more experienced people around you to learn from. If that's hard to accept, remember Confucius: "If you're the smartest person in the room, you're in the wrong room."
4. Treat work tasks as opportunities to build volume and develop your product skills step by step. Your workspace becomes a lab, and tasks become real-world practice. Solving real problems is far more engaging and valuable for professional growth than working through case studies. Just don't try to do it all at once — take it one step at a time. That steady accumulation of knowledge and skill is far more effective for reaching your goals than trying something once, getting burned, and stopping.

<h2>5. Following the Rules Without Question</h2>

Good managers are characterized by their intolerance of bureaucracy and formalism. Many people write documentation because "that's how it's done" — not because it helps them develop requirements more thoroughly. They follow "the rules" even when those rules slow product development down, instead of finding informal ways to reach their goals.

In short — they're afraid to break rules set by someone at some point in the past, or they fail to see opportunities to improve those rules. In doing so, they limit themselves, cutting off access to new and more effective approaches to problem-solving. Paul Graham pointed out that one of the important qualities of good work is "informality. Informality means focusing on what matters, not on what doesn't." [7]

**How to fix it?**

1. Develop critical thinking and mindfulness at work. Question any idea rather than accepting it at face value or deferring to established processes. Look for flaws in the logic of processes and in the arguments of the people around you. This requires focus, concentration, and energy — both physical and mental. At the same time, learn to distinguish healthy analysis and critique from mere fault-finding.
2. Develop your observational range. Don't hesitate to watch colleagues, ask questions, and analyze what's happening in other people's work. If you spot someone else's mistake, there's no need to call it out harshly — but discussing something that seems off or unclear is genuinely useful. Observing others helps you accumulate invaluable experience, as long as you approach it with goodwill rather than as a way to catch people out or assert yourself.

<h2>6. Prioritizing Form Over Substance</h2>

Junior PMs and students of PM courses often ask for templates of the perfect presentation, Product Roadmap, Customer Journey Map, or other work artifacts. But the point isn't the tools or the templates — it's the content. A great presentation can be put together in a notebook, and a Roadmap can be sketched in pencil on a piece of paper.

Writing coherent prose is harder than drawing a few boxes and connecting them with arrows. That's exactly why, in the work of junior managers and PM course students, you so often see "volumes of information" that are padded for quantity, that don't follow any logical structure, and that ultimately say nothing.

**How to fix it?**

1. Try to use the simplest possible tools so you can focus on content rather than on how things look. Don't expect the format to solve the problem — it's more likely to distract from what actually matters. Use a sheet of paper or a plain text document with no extra elements or visuals.
2. Let principles from adjacent engineering disciplines — which tend to be more rigorous and formal — guide your day-to-day product work. For example, "Less is more" [10] from software development, or "KISS (Keep It Short and Simple)" — a set of principles that originated in U.S. Navy engineering culture and has since taken firm root in software development and UX design.

<h2>7. Not Talking to Users</h2>

I often encounter product managers who don't study the users of the product they're building on their own. Some don't even use their own product, let alone talk to users — and this is true even at companies where there are dedicated UX researchers or entire research teams ready to help with study design or methodology.

A few years ago I read an article titled "From Managing Products To Managing Product Managers" [11] that significantly shaped my approach to working on new products. In it, the author gives a piece of advice I find wonderful: "A new product manager should know the product's users better than any other employee within the first 3 months on the job."

Understanding users allows you to become their advocate and to make decisions based on the actual problems and needs of the people you're building for. This is especially important for managers who have recently joined a team or product. Without it, a PM starts making decisions based on their own — or someone else's — assumptions, which is fatal for a product.

**How to fix it?**

1. Get to know the UX research team if your company has one, and find out directly from them what research methods they can help with. They can almost certainly help with study design as well.
2. Block out a fixed time window once a week to talk to users, and build that practice consistently: study and segment your audience, surface the problems they're running into, and develop a genuine understanding of their needs.

<h2>8. Not Studying Competitors</h2>

When building a new product, junior PMs often consider studying competitors' achievements and innovations unnecessary. This is a serious mistake. You need to "know your enemy by face," so to speak — to be prepared for healthy competition in the market for the user's attention.

A product manager who isn't watching the competition — which always exists — takes on a big risk of losing, because users may simply choose a different product that serves their needs better.

I'm not advocating mindlessly copying others' work, but checking your ideas against competitors — both locally and internationally — is always worth doing.

What strikes me most are situations where product managers delegate competitive analysis to the research team instead of doing it themselves. And it's equally disappointing when a manager ships a feature that's clearly worse than what key competitors already offer — simply because they couldn't be bothered to look at the market.

**How to fix it?**

1. Study the top 10 direct competitors by market share.
2. Become a user of your competitors' products, not just your own — experience them firsthand.
3. Subscribe to competitors' newsletters so you can stay on top of their promotions and new feature launches as they happen.
4. When designing new features, look at how competitors have solved the same problem.

<h2>9. Thinking in Constraints</h2>

I often see junior managers who can't think beyond existing limitations and only propose ideas that fit within established norms.

If a junior manager keeps thinking in constraints, within a couple of years they'll become an expert at making excuses and won't take on a single challenge. If they start thinking in terms of possibilities and ways to reach goals, they'll learn to imagine new products.

Boxed in by rules and self-imposed norms, a PM cuts off their own future — they won't be able to grow, build a career, or find satisfaction in their work.

**How to fix it?**

1. Make a conscious, deliberate effort to shed constraints. Look at a problem with fresh eyes and a clear head. That's when you'll start to see new paths and unconventional opportunities.
2. Be creative — don't be afraid to let your imagination run. Just don't get carried away to the point of becoming a dreamer with no grounding in reality. Remember: your goal is to envision and advance a product, but to do it in a non-obvious way.
3. Learn to surprise your target audience. Offer them not just a quality product, but deliver it in a way that isn't what they — or your competitors — would expect.
4. Don't be afraid of anything. Develop critical thinking. Compare your own progress over time with that of colleagues, draw conclusions, take the best of what you see — and always try to make it your own rather than simply copying it.

<h2>10. Thinking in Tasks</h2>

Junior product managers often think in terms of "executing a task" that a stakeholder has handed them, instead of identifying the root of the problem and either designing the most effective solution for it or formulating a set of hypotheses worth investigating.

The drive to avoid mindless task execution is the foundation of product culture and product thinking.

**How to fix it?**

1. Don't let yourself simply execute tasks set by others. "If you ever manage to do great work, it will probably be your own project" [7].
2. Be self-directed. Analyze the goals you're given, strive to solve them, and find your own path — even if those first attempts aren't your best.
3. Never rush to execute a task blindly. Give yourself time to think it through, and don't let anyone push you along when you know you haven't fully understood what's being asked.
4. Never propose unconsidered solutions — even when they're handed to you on a silver platter. Always rely on yourself, and never doubt your ability to figure things out.

Nobody walks into this profession and becomes successful from day one. Experience, skill, knowledge, and expertise are built gradually — through trial and error, which can always be corrected if you're willing. The road is made by walking.

Never despair, and always remember that nothing is impossible — because "the impossible just takes a little longer than the possible" (F. Nansen).
