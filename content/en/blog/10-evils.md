These "deadly sins" can serve as a self-assessment checklist for both beginner and more experienced managers.

So, here are the mistakes — and how to fix them.

<h2>1. Believing in multitasking</h2>

Every product manager faces enormous flows of information, including all manner of product development initiatives and ideas that no single person can possibly process within the time available. Stakeholders push their agendas, users complain, the calendar is packed with meetings, and the Product Backlog grows day by day.

Instead of focusing on the tasks that truly matter for the product, a beginning manager spreads their time and energy thinly across a wide range of goals. The core mistake is failing to understand that attention is a limited resource — one that isn't sufficient for handling every issue. This resource gets depleted both when we're actively pursuing a goal and when we're simply keeping a task loaded in our severely limited working memory.

It's worth remembering that "attention is a critically limited process. It becomes exhausted when additional tasks arise, and if those tasks are complex, it is exhausted quickly" [9]. Time management rests on two simple principles:

1. Start by writing down every unfinished task [1].
2. Every thought spinning around in our head consumes what Maxim Dorofeyev aptly calls "thought fuel." "If we can build habits that let us write things down instead of trying to remember them, we can live not just more effectively, but more calmly" [2].

The old proverb about chasing two hares and catching neither says exactly the same thing.

**How to fix it:**

1. Ruthlessly eliminate multitasking [11].
2. Accept the reality of your work: it's normal for stakeholders to want everything at once; it's normal to have a Product Backlog stretching a year or more into the future; it's normal to focus on what matters and not spend your attention on lower-priority items.
3. Build the habit of writing down all ideas and tasks — in text or visual form. The goal is to avoid losing information, avoid overloading your working memory, and stop wasting your attention resource needlessly.
4. Focus on the product strategy if one exists; if it doesn't (which also happens) — align on the key priorities for the quarter with your product lead. For requests that fall outside the product strategy, learn to say "no" politely.
5. Remember that effective work requires energy and a positive mindset, which depend directly on quality sleep, nutrition, physical activity, vitamins, and genuine rest in all its dimensions.
6. Keep in mind something that may seem obvious: if you invest time in learning time management tools and then pick a few that work best for you — say, the Pomodoro Technique or regular daily task lists — it will significantly improve your productivity.

<h2>2. Fear of making decisions</h2>

A product manager is the driving force behind a product's development, and experience, decisiveness, courage, and ambition largely determine whether a product succeeds in the market. Yet beginning managers are often afraid to make even decisions that have virtually no direct impact on the product strategy.

Indecisiveness, even in its mildest form, affects both the speed and quality of product development. And if a product manager fears making decisions even in high-stakes situations — or keeps deferring them, or offloads the responsibility onto others — this will inevitably lead to the collapse of the project and, more broadly, of their career [3].

Indecisiveness often hides behind the pretense of running yet another product experiment or validating yet another hypothesis. Instead of moving quickly and putting the product in front of users, there's an endless cycle of research framed as hypothesis testing for every question that comes up.

The product manager role demands certain qualities and knowledge that are acquired over time. But one of the key ingredients of a product manager's success is character — the ability to get things done, and to make mistakes early on as a way of building necessary experience.

A product manager should be able to assess their own strengths and weaknesses and work on the latter rather than burying the problem. For example, if someone is afraid of public speaking or struggles to hold their own at the negotiating table, they need to overcome that fear through training and self-work — and perhaps through working with a coach or therapist.

It's important to remember that experience is gained through trial and error. After making a poor decision, you'll face another important choice. And there's nothing wrong with being wrong again, because the process of accumulating knowledge is ongoing — the principle of quantity turning into quality — and professional mastery develops gradually.

**How to fix it:**

1. Keep the outcomes you're aiming for and the primary business goal in mind at all times. I've seen it many times — and fallen into the trap myself — where in the midst of a creative search for a solution, the original goal gets lost, and the solution found is "brilliant" but doesn't actually lead anywhere.
2. Different decisions carry different levels of consequence, and often "we can correct a mistake in a matter of hours. In other cases, the consequences can put the product or the entire business at risk" [4]. High-stakes decisions are best made in the morning, when your "thought fuel" or "psychic energy" [5] is replenished to its maximum.

<h2>3. Making decisions alone</h2>

The opposite extreme is making important product decisions unilaterally, without consulting colleagues or leadership. I've encountered situations more than once where a product manager made product decisions without discussing them with stakeholders — and only after the release did the flaws in those decisions surface, requiring rushed fixes.

**How to fix it:**

1. Don't be self-conscious about discussing problems and solutions with colleagues. Set your professional pride aside. Good advice will only help you, and there's no shame in turning to people who have experience in these matters and have been in situations similar to yours. These colleagues aren't just managers — more often than not, they're people in operational roles who sometimes have more hands-on experience with these things than your own boss [6]. Moreover, when decisions are made in close collaboration with the team, and disagreements are resolved through testing and discussion, a product manager signals to the team that their opinions are valued — which contributes to a healthy work environment [4].
2. Remember that any kind of help is good in moderation. Don't offload decision-making responsibilities onto colleagues or delegate your own authority to others. "Unnecessary help is harmful — meaning that if I ask someone for help to handle circumstances I should be handling myself, I seriously damage my belief in my own personal capabilities." [3] This is worth reflecting on when thinking about how to strike the right balance between accepting help and remaining self-sufficient.

<h2>4. Not giving 200% to the work</h2>

For a beginning product manager, it's important to be curious, to learn new things eagerly, and to build skills with enthusiasm — not just product-specific skills, but general management competencies as well.

That said, it's worth remembering that even the best courses are far from a guarantee of success. Knowledge acquired in training can become a trap for beginners in general, and product managers in particular — because what you're taught often differs fundamentally from what you'll actually be doing on the job.

I've met many people who were trained in "product romanticism" on product management courses, yet were completely unprepared for day-to-day work. In contrast, those who teach themselves and approach products with enthusiasm and fire in their eyes stand out noticeably.

So it's worth asking yourself: "Is this really something I want to be doing right now with 200% engagement — or not?" If the answer is no, don't waste your time — go find what inspires you.

If the answer is yes, remember: "Your work must have three qualities: it is something you have a natural aptitude for, something you are deeply interested in, and something that allows you to do excellent work" [7].

**How to fix it:**

1. Alexander Gorny once shared in an interview how working 80-hour weeks at Mail.Ru Group helped him build his career and learn to find genuine enjoyment in his professional life. He even recommended that every beginner try working at that pace for at least a year. There's real truth in that, so:
2. Set yourself an ambitious, bold professional goal — one that motivates you to be a relentless machine (first and foremost toward yourself) on the path to achieving it. The goal can be anything: getting a new role, making your product a market leader, becoming a head of product. Working 80 hours a week without a goal is hard labor; with a goal, it's just the routine of building toward a dream.
3. Recognize that your company has more experienced people you can learn from. If that's hard to accept, recall Confucius: "If you are the smartest person in the room, you are in the wrong room."
4. Treat work tasks as opportunities to build volume and develop your product skills step by step. Your workplace becomes your laboratory, and tasks become real-world skill practice. Solving real problems is far more engaging and useful for professional development than working through case studies. Don't try to take on everything at once — instead, follow the principle that the road is made by walking it. Building knowledge and skills gradually, little by little, is far more valuable and effective for reaching your goals than trying something once, getting burned, and stopping.

<h2>5. Following the rules without question</h2>

Good managers are distinguished by their intolerance of bureaucracy and formalism. For example, many people write documentation because "it's required and that's how things are done" — not to think through requirements more carefully. They stick to "the rules" even when it slows product development, instead of finding informal ways to achieve their goals.

In short, they're afraid to challenge rules established by someone, somewhere, at some point in the past — or they simply don't see opportunities to improve those rules. In doing so, they cut themselves off from finding new, more effective approaches to solving problems. Paul Graham noted that one of the important elements of good work is "informality. Informality means focusing on what matters rather than what doesn't." [7]

**How to fix it:**

1. Develop critical thinking and mindfulness in your work. Question and challenge every idea rather than blindly deferring to others' opinions or established workflows. Look for flaws in the logic of processes and in the arguments made by people you talk to. This requires being focused, collected, and mentally and physically energized. At the same time, learn to distinguish healthy analysis and critique from mere fault-finding.
2. Develop your powers of observation. Don't hesitate to watch how colleagues work, ask questions, and reflect on what you see. If you notice someone else's mistakes, there's no need to criticize them harshly — but discussing what seems wrong or unclear is genuinely valuable. Observing others builds invaluable experience, provided you approach it with goodwill rather than a desire to catch people out or prove yourself.

<h2>6. Prioritizing form over substance</h2>

Beginning product managers and course students often ask for templates for the perfect presentation, Product Roadmap, Customer Journey Map, or other work artifact. But the point isn't the tools or the templates — it's the content. A great presentation can be put together in a plain notebook, and a Roadmap can be sketched in pencil on a sheet of paper.

Writing coherent prose is harder than drawing a few boxes and connecting them with arrows. That's exactly why the work of beginning managers and management students so often features "volumes of information" that are padded for length, lack logical structure, and say very little.

**How to fix it:**

1. Try to use the simplest possible tools so you can focus on substance rather than presentation. Don't rely on visual form to carry your argument — it's more likely to distract from the point. Use a sheet of paper or a plain text document without unnecessary elements or graphics.
2. Let yourself be guided in day-to-day product work by principles from adjacent engineering fields — which, being more formal, have codified these ideas well. For example, "Less is more" [10] from software development, or "KISS (Keep It Short and Simple)" — a guiding principle that originated in U.S. Navy engineering culture and took root in software development and UX design.

<h2>7. Not talking to users</h2>

I frequently encounter product managers who don't study the users of the product they're working on independently. Some neither use their own product themselves nor speak with its users — despite the fact that their company employs dedicated specialists, or even entire UX research teams, who are always ready to help with process or advice.

A few years ago I read an article called "From Managing Products To Managing Product Managers" [11] that significantly influenced my approach to working on new products. In it, the author gives a wonderful piece of advice to beginning product managers: "A new product manager should know the product's users better than any other employee within the first 3 months on the job."

Understanding users enables you to become their advocate and make decisions grounded in the actual problems and interests of the people using your product. This is especially important for managers who have recently joined a team or product. Otherwise, the product manager starts making decisions based on their own assumptions or those of others — which is damaging for any product.

**How to fix it:**

1. Get to know the UX research team, if your company has one, and hear directly from them about the research methods they can help with. They'll almost certainly be able to help with research design as well.
2. Set aside a fixed time slot once a week for talking to users and build that practice consistently: study and segment your audience, gather the problems they encounter, and work to understand their needs.

<h2>8. Not studying competitors</h2>

When developing a new product, beginning managers often consider it unnecessary to study the achievements and discoveries of competitors. This is a serious mistake. Figuratively speaking, you need to "know your competitor by face" — to be ready for a healthy fight for customers in the market.

If a product manager isn't watching the competition — and there's always competition — they risk losing, because users may choose a different product that serves their needs more effectively.

I'm not advocating mindlessly copying others' work, but comparing your ideas against competitors — both locally and internationally — is never a bad idea.

What strikes me most are situations where product managers delegate competitive analysis to a research team rather than doing it themselves. It's equally frustrating when a manager ships a feature that's designed worse than what key competitors offer, simply because they didn't bother to study the market.

**How to fix it:**

1. Study the top 10 direct competitors of your product by market share.
2. Become a user not just of your own product, but of your competitors' products — learn from firsthand experience.
3. Subscribe to competitor newsletters so you can stay informed about their promotions and new feature releases in real time.
4. When designing new functionality, look at how competitors are solving the same problem.

<h2>9. Thinking in constraints</h2>

I often encounter situations where beginning managers can't break free from limitations and propose ideas only within the bounds of what already exists.

If a beginning manager keeps thinking in terms of constraints, within two or three years they'll become an expert in excuses and won't take on any task. If instead they think in terms of possibilities and paths to achieving goals, they'll learn to create new products.

A product manager who is boxed in by rules — and often by self-imposed norms — is cutting off their own future. They won't be able to grow, build a career, or find satisfaction in their work.

**How to fix it:**

1. Deliberately and consciously work to shed constraints. Approach problems with a fresh perspective and an open mind. This will allow you to see new paths forward and unconventional opportunities.
2. Be creative — don't be afraid to let your imagination run. But don't get carried away to the point of becoming a dreamer with your head in the clouds. Remember that your goal is to envision and advance a product, and to do so in a non-standard way.
3. Learn to surprise your target audience. Offer them not just a quality product, but do it in a way that's non-trivial — different from how your competitors do it.
4. Don't be afraid of anything. Develop your critical thinking. Compare your own progress over time and against your colleagues', draw conclusions, and take the best from what you see — but always make it your own rather than simply copying.

<h2>10. Thinking in tasks</h2>

Beginning product managers often operate in a "deliver the task" mode — implementing whatever a stakeholder has brought to them — instead of identifying the root of the problem and either designing the most effective solution for it or formulating a set of hypotheses to investigate.

The drive to avoid any kind of mindless task execution is the foundation of product culture and product thinking.

**How to fix it:**

1. Don't let yourself simply execute tasks set by others. "If you ever manage to do great work, it will probably be your own project" [7].
2. Be independent. Analyze the goals you've been given, strive to solve them, and find your own paths to doing so — even if those paths aren't the best ones at first.
3. Never rush into executing a task without thinking. Give yourself time to think it through, and don't let yourself be rushed when you feel you haven't fully understood what's being asked.
4. Never propose unconsidered solutions — even when someone brings them to you on a silver platter. Always rely on yourself and trust in your own abilities.

No one has ever entered a profession and become successful from their very first steps. Experience, ability, knowledge, and skills are built gradually, through trial and error — errors that, with effort, can be corrected. The road is made by walking it.

Never despair, and always remember that nothing is impossible — because "the impossible just takes a little longer than the possible" (F. Nansen).
